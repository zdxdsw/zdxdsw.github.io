Circle

This morning I finished reading Emerson's Circles. In the last paragraph he writes “The one thing which we seek with insatiable desire is to do something without knowing how or why, in short, to draw a new circle. 'A man,' said Oliver Cromwell, 'never rises so high as when he knows not whither he is going'.”

Oh gosh! “Someone who knows not whither they are going.” How easily we call that “getting lost”! There's such a stigma around not knowing where you're going. It saddens me how massively passion is discouraged in that way.

I think back to a meeting with my advisor. I told her that a paper of mine had just been accepted to a conference and it had started as a pure passion project. I was so happy for its acceptance. She endorsed it. Later in that meeting, she mentioned her 6-month sabbatical at Stanford. “It was great”, she said, “but I left several things unfinished there, and I haven't found time for them since I got back.” “Passion projects are great”, she went on, “but there are non-passion projects here that call for my attention and demand delivery to the grant.” This meeting was recalled because it was thought-provoking, not because I wanted to pick on what had been shared by her. I see the palpable tension between passion and responsibility, passion and reality, passion and priorities, passion and “shoulds”. It has almost led me to paint a picture where passion is against everything and everything is against passion.

As a pro-passion person walking the earth, I feel like I have to be a fighter, secretly, in a million moments.

Yet I don't think it has to be this confrontational. It doesn't have to be a gloomy either-or picture with “passion” cast as a kid being bullied at school by everyone else in the class. I don't want to give passion that seat in my life. Passion is not one kid in the classroom. Passion is the classroom.

What is non-passion? It can't mean irresponsibility. It can't mean unreality. It can't mean low-priorities. Non-passion is non-passion. It is another classroom, and it still has classmates. It is simply not a passion classroom.

In other words, passion is a property of the all-encompassing circle that contains responsibilities, priorities, duties, “shoulds”, and the mundane. Therefore, to add passion to life is to re-draw the circle rather than to prudently select what goes in and what stays out. Whether passion is a quality of the circle itself is a question largely orthogonal to which elements are members of the circle. As Emerson writes: “His only redress is forthwith to draw a circle outside of his antagonist.” In so many contexts these days, I get the sense that passion is ostracized as the laughable wish of kid in a stimulated moment. Inviting passion back into one's life can then feel like drawing a circle against everything that used to hold it back. Or, if we follow sequential thinking, it can feel like the things holding passion back should be dealt with in the first place. That framing gets the scale wrong. Passion is large. Members of the circle are orders of magnitude smaller.

Thinking about what to exclude to “make room for” passion risks giving in to the tyranny of the either-or question. When we try to draw a circle outside what we are tempted to treat as “anatagonists”, the either-or question loses its relevance, as it matters only at a smaller scale.
